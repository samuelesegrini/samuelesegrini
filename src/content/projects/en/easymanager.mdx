> EasyManager is where my path starts, and it is also what I am still working on. In April 2023, before university, I wrote in twenty commits an iPad manager for a real restaurant: order taking, kitchen, till, fiscal printing. In 2026 I came back to the same domain with what I had learned in between about distributed systems, hardware and verification, and rethought it as a modular Swift toolkit.
>
> The two eras share the problem, not the code. The public repository documents the first; the second is ongoing individual work, described here within the limits of what has been verified.

## A restaurant's problem

An order passes through four pairs of hands and three devices: the waiter who takes it at the table, the kitchen that prepares it by course, the till that closes it with one or more payments, the fiscal printer that turns it into a legally binding document. Each step has its own lifecycle and its own way of failing, and an error in the last one (a duplicate receipt, a wrong total) cannot be undone with a tap.

The client needed a single tool on iPad, with different roles for the staff and integration with the fiscal printer already in the dining room.

## The 2023 application

The app is pure SwiftUI with an MVVM architecture. Six `ObservableObject`s (`RestaurantViewModel`, `AuthenticationViewModel`, `OrderViewModel`, `ProductViewModel`, `PrinterViewModel`, `TableViewModel`) are created at launch and injected as `environmentObject`s into a view hierarchy organised by station: `DiningView` for the room, `OrdersView` and `OrderSheetView` for the order pad, `CookingView` for the kitchen, `CashDesk` with `CheckOut` and `ScontrinoView` for the till, and an admin area for products, categories, cover charges, tables and users.

Persistence is Cloud Firestore. Models are `Codable` structs with `@DocumentID`; view models open `addSnapshotListener` on the collections and rebuild local state on every snapshot, so several iPads in the room see the same order without an application server. Decoding distinguishes the four `DecodingError` cases and surfaces them as a message instead of silently dropping a malformed document.

The domain is richer than it looks. A table has a state (`free`, `booked`, `waiting`, `occupied`), an assigned waiter and a service start time. An order holds dishes with a course, a VAT rate, a decimal quantity, a void flag and variants with their own price; the total depends on which variants are selected. A user has roles and an operator number, which is what the fiscal printer demands on every line.

## The fiscal printer as an external system

The most instructive part is `PrinterViewModel`. The Epson printer in the room exposes the fiscal ePOS protocol over HTTP: the app composes an XML document inside a SOAP envelope and POSTs it to `fpmate.cgi`. I implemented two document types and the closing operations:

- the **pre-bill**, a non-fiscal document (`beginNonFiscal` … `endNonFiscal`) with one `printNormal` line per dish and variant and a total aligned on 46 columns, handed to the customer before paying;
- the **fiscal receipt** (`beginFiscalReceipt`), with one `printRecItem` per line carrying description, quantity, unit price and VAT department (three departments mapped onto the rates), and one `printRecTotal` per payment method: cash, cheque, electronic payment, meal voucher;
- the **X and Z reports** and the invoice from the last receipt, requested at the end of a shift.

This is where I first met a problem I would see again in distributed systems: the request leaves, the reply may never come, and not knowing whether the printer printed is not the same as knowing it did not. In 2023 the app recorded the connection error and left the decision to the operator. That is the starting point of the re-engineering.

## What the first era exposed

Scope grew with the client's requests (loyalty, reservations, shifts, inventory) and the seams became visible: a restaurant operation is not one screen, and every area has its own timing and failure modes. Keeping everything in six shared view models made local changes carry consequences elsewhere. That is the useful evidence from 2023: the domain vocabulary and the pressure points. It is not proof that the app had already solved hardware compatibility or fiscal certification.

## The re-engineering

The 2026 work asks a narrower question: which boundaries let this workflow remain understandable when an order outlives a view, a device answers late and a fiscal mutation can be uncertain?

The answer is a set of Swift 6.3 packages separated by contract: order domain, device lanes, fiscal layer, SQL persistence at the boundary, DocC documentation next to every versioned API. The 0.1.0 API documents and builds 31 products with no remote dependencies; feature code depends on focused domain and hardware contracts rather than on a service surface modelled after the application.

Three design decisions hold the shape:

- **An order is a durable state machine.** Draft, sent, acknowledged, reconciled: outbox, acknowledgement and reconciliation are separate phases, and a timeout preserves uncertainty for the next step instead of silently retrying a mutation that could duplicate a fiscal document.
- **Hardware is not just another API.** A per-device lane serialises access to a physical endpoint with Swift Concurrency, because two individually valid commands become unsafe when interleaved.
- **The fiscal layer may answer "uncertain".** An incomplete outcome is a domain value, not an error to hide.
