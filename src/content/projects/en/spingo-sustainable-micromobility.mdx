> SpinGO is the 30%usability group project for the 2025–2026 Human-Computer Interaction course at Politecnico di Milano. Five of us went through the whole user-centred process: needfinding, needs synthesis, prototypes of increasing fidelity, cross-team heuristic evaluation, usability testing and final delivery.
>
> In my path it is the project that put research before code. After distributed systems and hardware, the question here was not how to build another navigation app, but what keeps people from adopting these vehicles consistently and how an interface can give control back without adding distraction. The design is collective work; the website that gathers the research, seven deliverables and the prototypes is mine.

## The domain and the research question

We chose urban micromobility because its environmental potential collides with a fragile everyday experience. The pool of needs was visible before the data: discontinuous lanes, theft, unreliable parking, fragmented information. The research question became: which barriers turn a convenient choice into a decision loaded with uncertainty, and which of them can a digital product actually lower?

The target was split into three profiles: average users who own or share a vehicle but ride less than they would like, lead users who ride daily for work or commuting, and domain experts (associations, sustainable-mobility advocates) for a systemic view.

## Needfinding: questionnaire and interviews

The questionnaire was built by decomposing abstract concepts such as safety and comfort into observable factors (traffic, lighting, parking conditions, weather), with Likert scales for perceptions, multiple choice for behaviour and open questions for experiences. Questions followed the rider's journey (before, during, after) and the path adapted to the declared profile: experts, interested but inactive, not interested.

We collected 109 responses. 95.4% of the sample is aged 18–35 and 78% lives in Milan or its province, consistent with the urban context under study. 43.1% identified as expert users; among the others, 74.2% are interested but inactive and 25.8% not interested. That figure steered the project: latent demand is larger than actual use.

Four semi-structured interviews of 30–45 minutes, run in person by an interviewer–note-taker pair, went deep on deliberately different profiles: a professional rider who picks a small, unremarkable bike as an anti-theft strategy, a scooter commuter with insufficient range and no public charging, a student who uses bike sharing and will not buy a bike for fear of theft, a graduate who bought a bike and never used it. Profiles were compared on six dimensions (usage intensity, experience, dependence on the vehicle, risk exposure, problem awareness, workaround capacity) to measure the real impact of the barriers.

## From data to needs

Interviews and two focus groups were analysed with open, axial and selective coding. Over 150 raw codes converged into thematic clusters and then into a board of more than fifty granular needs; affinity mapping and dot voting brought the group to four directions: safety and control, trust in places and information, fluidity across the phases of a trip, and belonging to a community that turns individual reports into collective knowledge.

## Three tasks, two directions, one companion

Three tasks drove the design: plan and start a safe route, manage vehicles and preferences (the "Garage"), interact with the reporting community. In the low-fidelity phase we compared a dedicated handlebar device with a native mobile app. The app covered the three tasks better but exposed riders to distraction; the final solution keeps the richness of the app and moves the essential interactions to a smartwatch companion: next turn, haptic feedback, quick reporting.

The cross-team heuristic evaluation with another group (GreenBytes) surfaced a tab bar that was too small, a back button outside convention, actions without feedback and ambiguity between points of interest and saved routes. Before testing we replaced static maps with real data, enlarged and clarified navigation, added labels and confirmations and repositioned controls to mobile standards.

## Usability testing

The high-fidelity Figma prototype was evaluated with seven participants aged 16–35, in person or remotely, in sessions of about 45 minutes combining think-aloud, observation, time and errors, a Single Ease Question after each task and the System Usability Scale at the end. Target metrics were a success rate above 85%, SUS above 70 and fewer than three severity 1–2 problems.

Effectiveness was scored on a 0–2 scale per task (complete success, minor problems, major problems, failure) against a 14-point maximum. The average success rate is 88%: 93% for navigation (SEQ 6.5/7), 82% for personalisation (SEQ 4.2/7), 89% for community (SEQ 5.8/7). The mean SUS is 89.2, grade A: six users out of seven between 82.5 and 100, one outlier at 70 traced to confusion between route and point-of-interest symbols. The mean reaction time to hazards in the navigation task was 7.8 seconds.

Problems ranked by severity say more than the average. 28% of users failed saved-route management because the information architecture separated viewing (Home) from managing (Profile), a distinction that does not exist for the user: severity 1. In the Garage two users edited an existing vehicle instead of adding a new one, because the "Add" command sat at the bottom of the list: severity 2. Redundant "Start" buttons and manual entry of the starting point instead of the current position are severity 3.

The test was not a final confirmation but a new source of decisions: unify viewing and managing, move the create action to the top, use smart defaults for the start, differentiate route and point icons.

## The project website

I built the site that gathers the research, seven deliverables, videos of the final prototype and the Figma prototypes: React 19 with TypeScript, Vite 7, GSAP with ScrollTrigger for the horizontal deliverables timeline and section transitions, dedicated components for personas, values and a showcase with a modal for the prototypes, published to GitHub Pages through GitHub Actions. I authored 112 of the repository's 119 commits.

## What I carry forward

A positive number does not close a piece of research. The SUS of 89.2 describes an overall perception; one user's 45 seconds stalled in front of a panel that would not let them edit describes a mental model the interface does not mirror. In a new iteration I would validate the corrected flows in a working demo, reduce the site's non-essential animation and add systematic support for keyboard, focus and reduced motion.
